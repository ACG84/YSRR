A magnonic reservoir that remembers but does not compute

Simulation has taken the reservoir as far as it usefully can, and has just answered the question that was going to be asked of you. The proposed rescue — a delay bus with taps — failed because nothing propagates on the bus at 12 GHz. What remains is one prior question about dispersion that decides whether any delay-line architecture is worth building here.

The bottom line

A vortex-disk magnonic reservoir was built and benchmarked on NARMA-10. It has ample linear memory — recall of the input out to lag 15, memory capacity 15.7 — and essentially no nonlinear capacity: 98–99% of measured information processing capacity is degree-1, and the cross-lag product the task actually needs sits at exactly 0.000.

It therefore loses to a 20-tap linear filter: 0.1905 against 0.1243. Five mechanisms and three coupling architectures have been tried and measured out.

The rescue was to build the missing products explicitly: a spin-wave bus carrying the input past a row of taps, each multiplying a fresh sample against a delayed one. That is now measured out too, and for a prior reason — the bus does not propagate.

The device

Permalloy, 20 nm thick, 5 nm cells, LLG integrated with RK4 (magnum.np). A 200 nm vortex disk with six 80 nm radial waveguide ports; readout is a per-port lock-in at five tones plus a cross-port DFT, which is physically the azimuthal mode decomposition. Input is amplitude modulation of a 12 GHz carrier between 10 and 30 mT, one frame per sample, 200 steps (0.2 ns) per frame.

What has been ruled out

Three campaigns. The first sought more memory; the second sought the nonlinear mixing that memory turned out not to supply; the third tried to build that mixing explicitly out of a delay bus, and is the subject of the next section.

Why every tap architecture failed the same way

They didn't fail on coupling, and they didn't fail on damping. The bus is not a delay line. At 12 GHz in this geometry nothing propagates along it; the taps are reading an evanescent field that falls off over a few hundred nanometres and arrives everywhere at once.

The test that settled it: keep the four taps at their 3-frame spacing and move the whole array ten frames further from the injection, at a bus damping where a real guided wave would lose 6% over the extra 1.89 µm. Three predictions were registered before the run.

The evanescent number is not a fit to this data. Its decay length was derived from the amplitude spread within the unshifted array, and then predicted an absolute amplitude 1.89 µm away in a separately built and separately relaxed mesh.

The observation that closes it

Attenuation makes a wave small. It does not make it punctual. Across the array the four arrival times are 12.91, 13.00, 13.00, 12.95 frames — flat to 0.04 frames over 1701 nm, where 945 m/s demands that tap 4 lag tap 1 by 9.0. A loaded or lossy bus would still deliver the signal late. This one delivers it on time everywhere, so it did not travel.

Every knob turned over the last several sweeps — coupling gap, directional couplers, tap damping ratio, bus damping — was adjusting how much of a signal that never propagated reached the taps.

What we need from you

One question, and it comes before any architecture.

The question — dispersion

Is 12 GHz inside the propagating spin-wave band of a 20 nm × 80 nm permalloy strip in this configuration at all? And if not, what combination of bias field, strip width, and drive frequency puts a genuinely propagating mode on a bus at a group velocity that gives useful delay per unit length?

The competing explanation we cannot separate from our own data: the strip may propagate fine and our launcher may be at fault — a 100 nm out-of-plane field segment may simply couple to nothing that carries. Either answer is decisive, and they point at different fixes.

Why we are not asking what we were going to ask

The previous version of this brief asked whether a 10:1 Gilbert damping ratio between capped and bare film was realistic, on the strength of a design equation for how many taps fit within a given dynamic range. That equation is refuted — it assumed the tap-to-tap spread came from propagation loss, and a thirtyfold longer attenuation length left the spread at 405×, 458×, 408×, 388×. Differential damping does do the one thing it was claimed to do, ordering taps that were previously unordered, but on a bus with nothing travelling on it that buys nothing. The materials question was the wrong question.

Caveats, stated plainly

  • The evanescent decay is not one clean exponential. Fitted span by span it runs 196, 354, 386, 438, 638, 539, 1283 nm — softening with distance, as a short-range field plus a weak long-range remainder would. A single exponential over all eight taps gives κ = 426 nm with a 3.7× worst residual. The scale is solid; the functional form is not.
  • Two published constants of this project are now suspect and struck through above: the 951 nm bus attenuation length and the 945 m/s group velocity. Both came from the same kind of measurement on the same kind of field, and the velocity came from the threshold arrival estimator that has already produced one false delay here. Note the direction of the inconsistency — the decay measured inside the array is ~400 nm at a thirtieth the damping, shorter than the 951 nm measured at full damping. Propagation loss cannot do that.
  • Three claims in the project record were wrong and have been corrected: that the device operated in linear response (it does not — the disk is strongly nonlinear across the whole operating window); that tap position set tap delay (it never did; that rested on an arrival estimator that fires on the first thing above threshold); and that tap-to-tap amplitude spread followed from the damping ratio (refuted by the bus-damping sweep, and this brief previously carried that claim as its central design equation).
  • NARMA-10's difficulty is not the u[n−9]·u[n] term everyone quotes. Adding it to a linear filter changes 0.1243 to 0.1242. The value is in the whole family of long-separation products.
