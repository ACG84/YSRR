A magnonic reservoir that remembers but does not compute

The reservoir has ample memory and no nonlinear capacity, and loses to a linear filter. The rescue — a spin-wave delay bus tapped at many distances — spent four campaigns failing. It was never broken. Every run ended before the wave reached the far taps; with a long enough rollout the unmodified array resolves all four delays and clears the detection threshold fourfold.

The bottom line

A vortex-disk magnonic reservoir was built and benchmarked on NARMA-10. It has ample linear memory — recall of the input out to lag 15, memory capacity 15.7 — and essentially no nonlinear capacity: 98–99% of measured information processing capacity is degree-1, and the cross-lag product the task actually needs sits at exactly 0.000.

It therefore loses to a 20-tap linear filter: 0.1905 against 0.1243. Five mechanisms and three coupling architectures have been tried and measured out.

The rescue was to build the missing products explicitly: a spin-wave bus carrying the input past a row of taps, each multiplying a fresh sample against a delayed one. That now works in simulation — four taps at 3.46, 1.20 and 3.76 frames of separation against a designed 3.00, all monotonic. Whether resolved taps actually supply the long-separation products the task needs is untested.

The device

Permalloy, 20 nm thick, 5 nm cells, LLG integrated with RK4 (magnum.np). A 200 nm vortex disk with six 80 nm radial waveguide ports; readout is a per-port lock-in at five tones plus a cross-port DFT, which is physically the azimuthal mode decomposition. Input is amplitude modulation of a 12 GHz carrier between 10 and 30 mT, one frame per sample, 200 steps (0.2 ns) per frame.

Why every tap architecture failed: the run ended before the wave arrived

Four suspects were measured and cleared — the band contains 12 GHz, the launcher works, the loaded bus carries the wave past every tap with phase R² = 1.000, and the bus field varies only 5.1× across the array where the taps reported 405×. That last gap looked like a coupling failure. It was not.

The actual cause

Every poly-tap run used a rollout of 2.81 ns. At the measured group velocity the wave needs 3.75 ns to reach tap 3 and 6.42 ns to reach tap 4. The simulation ended before the signal arrived, so the taps were reading injection near field — which is instantaneous, which is why all four reported the same arrival time, and why that time was the disk's own response rather than anything to do with distance.

Nothing in the output looked wrong. Amplitudes were plausible, the estimator returned finite lags, and those lags were reproducible across runs and geometries. That reproducibility read as reliability. It was the signature of a quantity that did not depend on the thing being varied.

What the ladder says about improving it

With a valid record, every modification we tried is a loss on timing, a gain on brightness, or both. Nothing beats the device as built on the quantity the architecture actually needs.

Bus damping buys amplitude and costs timing, exactly in line with the wave being damping-limited (vg/αω = 1170 nm predicted against 1354 nm measured). Only the as-built point gives both 3/3 monotonic and a mean spacing near the designed 3.0.

An earlier draft of this brief blamed the tap's coupling aperture, on the arithmetic that an 80 nm guide sampling an 82.3 nm wave sits on its own sinc null. That is refuted. At matched damping the 80 nm aperture beats 40 nm at every tap, with per-tap ratios of 2.5, 1.1, 1.6 and 3.0 for a width ratio of exactly 2 — coupling proportional to width, no null. The supporting evidence for the null (a non-monotonic optimum at W ≈ λ/2, confirmed out of sample) was measured on the truncated record: it was the response curve of near-field pickup against aperture width, which is a real curve and the wrong one.

What we need from you

The delay line works, so the questions are about whether it scales and whether our numbers are believable.

Question 1 — sanity-check the delay line

On an unbiased 80 × 20 nm permalloy strip at 12 GHz we measure λ = 82.3 nm (phase slope, R² = 1.000), a band bottom near 10.5 GHz, local vg = 706 m/s rising to 1267 at 20 GHz, and a propagation length of 1354 nm on the loaded bus against vg/αω = 1170 nm. Are those the right order for this geometry, and is an exchange-dominated forward branch what you would expect with k ∥ M and no applied field?

Question 2 — how many taps does this scale to?

Four taps span 1.7 µm at 567 nm spacing, with a 29× amplitude spread end to end and a propagation length of 1.35 µm. The task needs of order 40 independent product dimensions. Lowering bus damping flattens the array beautifully (29× → 2.8×) but scatters the timing. Is there a principled operating point that keeps both — or is the honest answer that a magnonic delay line tops out at a handful of resolvable taps?

Question 3 — the dispersion penalty

The packet is ~5 GHz wide (one sample per 0.2 ns frame) and vg varies from 706 to 1267 m/s across that span, so arrivals should smear by roughly 3 frames over the array. We see spacings scatter (3.46, 1.20, 3.76 where 3.00 was designed) and cannot yet attribute it. Does dispersive spreading put a hard floor on tap density here, and is there a standard mitigation?

Questions we are no longer asking, and why

Earlier drafts of this brief asked, in turn, whether a 10:1 Gilbert damping ratio was fabricable, whether 12 GHz was in the band, and how to couple to an 82 nm wave through a stub sitting on its sinc null. All three were wrong questions built on the same broken measurement, and all three are kept in the record below rather than quietly dropped.

Caveats, stated plainly

  • The device's field is two superposed components, and nearly all of this project's confusion came from measuring their sum too early. Injection near field: ~400 nm decay, geometric, damping-independent, instantaneous. The guided wave: 1354 nm decay on the loaded bus, damping-limited, arriving 1.3–3.8 ns later. Truncated runs saw only the first.
  • The 945 m/s group velocity is vindicated, twice over. A phase-slope fit over 12–20 GHz gives 948 m/s, and the measured arrivals imply 1010 m/s — mean spacing 2.81 frames against 3.00 designed, a 6% error. An earlier draft struck this constant through, and a later one claimed the taps were 34% too far apart because the envelope should travel at the local 706 m/s. Both were wrong: subtracting transit at 948 m/s leaves a constant 7.8-frame disk response across all four taps, while 706 m/s leaves a systematically falling residual. The burst is one frame wide, so it spans ~5 GHz and travels at the broadband velocity.
  • The 951 nm attenuation length is still unreconciled — the dispersion run gives 3252 nm on a bare strip at 5 mT against 951 nm from a CW fit at 30 mT, where the disk is known to compress 24%.
  • Five claims in the project record were wrong and are corrected in place rather than removed: that the device operated in linear response; that tap position set tap delay; that tap spread followed from the damping ratio; that nothing propagated at 12 GHz; and that the tap aperture sat on a sinc null that explained the failure. The last three all trace to the same truncated rollout.
  • Two of those failures share a shape worth naming. The 1000-step dispersion record returned 275 nm where the correct run gives 3252 nm — and 275 nm agreed beautifully with the ~285 nm the tap array was showing. The aperture sweep produced a non-monotonic optimum exactly where theory predicted it. In both cases a broken measurement reproduced the expected answer, which is the circumstance in which a broken measurement is least likely to be caught.
  • NARMA-10's difficulty is not the u[n−9]·u[n] term everyone quotes. Adding it to a linear filter changes 0.1243 to 0.1242. The value is in the whole family of long-separation products.
